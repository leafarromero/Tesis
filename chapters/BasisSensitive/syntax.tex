\section{Core language}\label{sec:calculus}
\subsection{Syntax and congruence}
This section presents the calculus on which our realisability model will be
built. It is a $\lambda$-calculus extended with linear combinations of terms,
along the lines of the language Lineal \cite{ArrighiDowekLMCS17}.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{rcll}
    $v$ & $::=$ & $x \mid \Lam{x}{X}{\vec{t}} \mid (v, v) \mid \ket{0} \mid \ket{1}$ & $(\Val)$ \\
    $t$ & $::=$ & $v \mid tt \mid (t,t) \mid \LetP{x}{X}{y}{Y}{t}{\vec{t}} \mid \gencase{t}{\vec{v}}{\vec{v}}{\vec{t}}{\vec{t}}$ & $(\Lambda)$ \\
    $\vec{v}$ & $::=$ & $v \mid \vec{v}+\vec{v} \mid \alpha \vec{v} \mid \vec 0 \qquad (\alpha\in\C)$ & $(\ValD)$ \\
    $\vec{t}$ & $::=$ & $t \mid \vec{t}+\vec{t} \mid \alpha \vec{t} \mid \vec 0 \qquad (\alpha\in\C)$ & $(\vec \Lambda)$
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Syntax of the calculus, where $X, Y \subseteq \ValD$.}
  \label{tab:Syntax}
\end{table}
The syntax of the calculus is described in
Table~\ref{tab:Syntax}; it is divided into four
distinct syntactic categories: \emph{basic values} ($\Val$), \emph{basic terms}
($\Lambda$), \emph{value combinations} ($\ValD$), and \emph{term
combinations} ($\vec\Lambda$). Values consist of variables, decorated
lambda abstractions, and two basis values representing orthogonal vectors,
$\ket 0$ and $\ket 1$. A pair of values is also considered a value.  Terms
include values, applications, pair constructors and destructors, and pattern
matching for orthogonal vectors, represented by the $\mathsf{case}$ operator.
This $\mathsf{case}$ operator supports quantum control, which enables
computations without a definite causal structure, such as the quantum switch 
\cite{Chiribella_2013}.

Both term and value combinations are built as $\C$-linear combinations of
basic terms and values, respectively. We use $v,u,w$ to denote values and
$t,s,r$ for terms, writing $\vec{\cdot}$ when can be combinations.

The decorations $X$, and $Y$ appearing in the abstractions and pair
destructors denote either the basis of the vector spaces in which the terms
are expressed or a mark for a higher-order variable; their precise nature is
made explicit later. Before formally defining the notion of basis, we first
establish the algebraic structure underlying the space of value combinations.
This is achieved by introducing the congruence relation defined in
Table~\ref{tab:Congruence}.
This relation captures the intended behaviour of addition and scalar
multiplication, allowing us to treat linear combinations of terms and values
as genuine algebraic entities rather than mere syntactic constructions.
In particular, it enforces the commutativity and associativity of addition and
the distributivity of scalar multiplication, thereby justifying the use of summation
notation~$\sum$.

\begin{table}[t]
  \begin{align*}
    0\vec t &\equiv \vec 0 & \vec t+\vec 0 &\equiv \vec t \\
    1\vec{t} &\equiv \vec{t} &
    \alpha(\beta\vec{t}) &\equiv (\alpha\beta)\vec{t} \\
    \vec{t}_{1}+\vec{t}_{2} &\equiv \vec{t}_{2}+\vec{t}_{1} &
    (\vec{t}_{1}+\vec{t}_{2})+\vec{t}_{3} &\equiv \vec{t}_{1}+(\vec{t}_{2}+\vec{t}_{3}) \\
    (\alpha+\beta)\vec{t} &\equiv \alpha\vec{t}+\beta\vec{t} &
    \alpha(\vec{t}_{1}+\vec{t}_{2}) &\equiv \alpha\vec{t}_{1}+\alpha\vec{t}_{2}\\
    \end{align*}
  \caption{Term congruence}
  \label{tab:Congruence}
\end{table}

The rules in Table~\ref{tab:Congruence} ensure that the set of value combinations
satisfies the axioms of a vector space. The notion of basis in this calculus builds
on this algebraic foundation. Once the vector structure is established, we can define
which subsets $B\subseteq \ValD$ qualify as \emph{bases}, thereby justifying the
decorations appearing in the syntax of Table~\ref{tab:Syntax}.

We will also consider the notation in Table~\ref{tab:Macros} for linear
combinations of terms. These expressions do not appear in the syntax,
yet they are useful to describe specific states. A simple example is the linear
combination of pairs where we can express the following state:
\[
\frac{1}{2}\Pair{\ket{0}+\ket{1}}{\ket{0}-\ket{1}} :=
\frac{1}{2}(\Pair{\ket{0}}{\ket{0}}- \Pair{\ket{0}}{\ket{1}}
+\Pair{\ket{1}}{\ket{0}}-\Pair{\ket{1}}{\ket{1}})
\]
As the tuple grows in dimension, the number of subterms in the combination grow
exponentially. The introduced notation allows for a more compact representation.

\begin{table}[t]
  \begin{align*}
    \Pair{\alpha  t+\vec{s}}{\vec{r}} 
    &:= \alpha\Pair{t}{\vec{r}} + \Pair{\vec{s}}{\vec{r}} &
    \Pair{t}{\alpha s+\vec{r}} 
    &:= \alpha\Pair{t}{s} + \Pair{t}{\vec{r}}\\
    (\alpha t+\vec{s})\vec{r} &:= \alpha(t \vec{r})+\vec{s}\vec{r} &
    t(\alpha s+\vec{r}) &:= \alpha (t s)+ t \vec{r}
  \end{align*}
  \vspace{-2\baselineskip}
  \begin{align*}
    \LetP{x_1}{X}{x_2}{Y}{\alpha t+\vec{s}}{\vec{r}}
    &:= \alpha(\LetP{x_1}{X}{x_2}{Y}{t}{\vec{r}})
      +(\LetP{x_1}{X}{x_2}{Y}{\vec{s}}{\vec{r}}) 
      \\
    \gencasei{(\alpha t+\vec{s})}{\vec{v}_{i}}{\vec{r}_{i}}
    &:= \alpha(\gencasei{t}{\vec{v}_i}{\vec{r}_i}) + 
      \gencasei{\vec{s}}{\vec{v}_i}{\vec{r}_i}
  \end{align*}
  \caption{Syntax sugar for term combinations}
  \label{tab:Macros}
\end{table}

Before proceeding further, let us briefly illustrate the intuition behind the
congruence in Table~\ref{tab:Congruence}.  
The key idea is that arguments are decomposed over the bases associated with
their corresponding abstractions.  
As in linear algebra, a vector can be rewritten as a linear combination of
basis elements; for instance,
\(
  (1,0)
  = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\!\left(\tfrac{(1,1)}{\sqrt{2}}
  + \tfrac{(1,-1)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
\)
expresses $(1,0)$ in the basis
$\{\tfrac{(1,1)}{\sqrt{2}}, \tfrac{(1,-1)}{\sqrt{2}}\}$.
By identifying $(1,0)$ with $\ket{0}$ and $(0,1)$ with $\ket{1}$, we see that
the calculus allows every vector (or value combination) to be expressed as a
superposition of elements of the basis attached to each abstraction.
The congruence ensures that these linear combinations behave algebraically as
in a complex vector space, supporting the standard operations of addition,
scalar multiplication, and the zero vector.
On its original presentation, Lineal \cite{ArrighiDowekLMCS17} and following
refinements \cite{DiazcaroDowekRinaldiBIO19,DiazcaroMonzonAPLAS25}, implemented
these operations via a set of directed reduction rules with defined normal forms
and additional casting rules. This approach lends itself to implementation, however
the resulting system is cumbersome to analyze under a realisability framework.
For this reason we choose to represent the vector space identities using a
congruence relation.


To properly characterise the sets that decorate the lambda abstractions, we
must first specify the kind of values they contain.
\begin{definition}[Qubits]\label{def:Qubit}
  A \emph{1-qubit state} is a value combination of the form
  $\alpha\ket{0} + \beta\ket{1}$, where $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$. An
  \emph{$n$-qubit state} is a value combination of the form
  $\alpha\Pair{\ket{0}}{\vec{w}_{1}} + \beta\Pair{\ket{1}}{\vec{w}_{2}}$, where
  $\vec{w}_{1}$ and $\vec{w}_{2}$ are $(n-1)$-qubit states,
  where $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$.
\end{definition}
We use the usual Dirac shorthand $\ket{xy}$ for $(\ket{x},\ket{y})$, and
extend it to longer tuples by associating to the right.

From now on, we call the value combinations, i.e. the elements of $\ValD$,
\emph{vectors}. The vector space $\ValD$ is equipped with an inner
product defined by,  
\(
  \scal{\vec{v}}{\vec{w}} := \sum_{i=1}^k\sum_{j=1}^k
  \overline{\alpha_i}\,\beta_j\,\delta_{v_i,w_j},
\)
and an $\ell_2$-norm 
\(
  \|\vec{v}\| := \sqrt{\scal{\vec{v}}{\vec{v}}}
  = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^k|\alpha_i|^2}
\),
where $\vec{v}=\sum_{i=1}^k\alpha_i v_i$ and
$\vec{w}=\sum_{j=1}^k\beta_j w_j$, and $\delta_{v_i,w_j}$ is the Kronecker
delta, equal to $1$ if $v_i=w_j$ and $0$ otherwise.

With this notion of inner product, we can complete the description of the
calculus syntax. As expected, two values are \emph{orthogonal} when their inner
product is equal to zero. We can now formally define the sets that
decorate abstractions.
\begin{definition}[Basis]\label{def:NthDimensionalBasis}
  A set of value combinations $\mathbb{B}$ is a \emph{$2^n$-dimensional orthonormal
  basis} if it satisfies:
  \begin{enumerate*}
    \item $\mathbb{B}$ has cardinality $2^n$.
    \item Each element of $\mathbb{B}$ is an $n$-qubit state \emph{(cf.~Definition~\ref{def:Qubit})}.
    \item Distinct elements of $\mathbb{B}$ are pairwise orthogonal.
  \end{enumerate*}
\end{definition}

As a first step, the decorations $X$ and $Y$ introduced in Table~\ref{tab:Syntax}
can be restricted to sets forming $2^n$-dimensional
orthonormal bases. These sets indicate the basis in which a term is expressed:
qubits in the decorating basis will be treated in call-by-value manner, while
others are decomposed as $\C$-linear combinations of basis elements, with the
function applied linearly to each component. The basis analysis will be
restricted to $n$-qubit states. We will examine later the handling for
higher-order terms.


As in classical linear algebra, no non-trivial linear combination of basis
elements yields the null vector; otherwise, some basis vector would violate
pairwise orthogonality. Consequently, each decomposition over a basis is
unique.

\begin{remark}[Unique decomposition]\label{rem:UniqueDecomposition}\label{cor:EquivalentDecomposition}
  If $B$ is a $2^n$-dimensional basis, then every $n$-qubit state has a
  unique decomposition over $B$. This means that when $\vec{v} \equiv \vec{w}$,
  then they share the same decomposition over any basis $B$.
  \qed
\end{remark}

We denote the computational basis by $\B=\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}$ and the Hadamard
basis by $\XB=\{\ket{+},\ket{-}\}$, where
$\ket{\pm}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}\pm\ket{1})$.  

\subsection{Basis-dependent substitutions}
As usual, the expression $\vec t\ [\vec v/x]$ denotes the
capture-avoiding substitution of $\vec v$ for $x$ in $\vec t$.
However, beta-reduction depends on the basis chosen for the abstraction, so we must
define a substitution that takes this mechanism into account. Intuitively, this
operation substitutes variables with vectors expressed in the chosen basis; the
accompanying coefficients are those of the value combination being
substituted.

Alongside this substitution, we extend the set of possible basis annotations to
higher-order by including a special symbol \AbsBasis. Since we only consider bases
for first-order terms, we need a method to deal with combinations of 
$\lambda$-abstractions. This symbol marks that the argument of a function should be
treated linearly over its basic values as defined in the syntax. So, from now on, 
we will use $X, Y, Z$ (as in Table \ref{tab:Syntax}) to refer to either
$2^n$-dimensional othonormal bases, or the special symbol $\AbsBasis$ and
refer them as \emph{decorations}. When we refer exclusively to orthonormal bases,
excluding $\AbsBasis$, we will use $\BB$.

\begin{definition}[Basis-dependent substitution]\label{def:basisDependentSub}
  Let $\vec t$ be a term combination, $\vec v$ a value combination, $x$ a
  variable, and $X$ an orthonormal basis or $\AbsBasis$. The substitution 
  $\vec t\,\ansubst{\vec v/x}{X}$ is defined as follows:
  \[
  \vec t\,\ansubst{\vec v/x}{X}\;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \ds\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_i\vec{t}\;[\vec{b}_i/x] &
    \text{If } X=\{\vec{b}_i\}_{i\in I}\text{ and }
    \vec{v}\equiv\sum_{i\in I} \alpha_i\vec{b}_i\\[6pt]
    \ds\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_i\vec{t}\;[v_i/x] &
    \text{If }X=\AbsBasis\text{ and }
    \vec{v}=\sum_{i\in I} \alpha_i v_i\\[2pt]
    \text{undefined} & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
  \]
  
  As remarked in \ref{rem:UniqueDecomposition}, this decomposition
  is unique, and the substitution is well-defined.
\end{definition}

This definition captures two substitution modes: linear decomposition of $\vec v$
over an explicit basis $X$, or over its basic values when $X=\AbsBasis$
(recovering the mechanism of~\cite{ArrighiDowekLMCS17}). The latter is the only
case applicable to $\lambda$-abstractions.

The operation extends to pairs: if
$\vec v\equiv\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_i(\vec{v}_i, \vec{w}_i)$, then $\vec t
\ansubst{\vec v/x\otimes y}{X\otimes Y} = \sum_{i\in I}\alpha_i (\vec t
\ansubst{\vec{v}_i/x}{X}\ansubst{\vec{w}_i/y}{Y})$, where $X, Y$ are
the bases associated with $x, y$.

\begin{example}
  Let
  $\vec v = \alpha\ket{01}
           + \beta\ket{10}$.
  Then 
  \[
    (y,x)\ansubst{\vec v/x\otimes y}{\B\otimes\B}
    = \alpha\,(y,x)[\ket{0}/x][\ket{1}/y]
    + \beta\,(y,x)[\ket{1}/x][\ket{0}/y] 
    = 
    \alpha\ket{10}
    + \beta\ket{01}
  \]
\end{example}

With this substitution in place, we can establish certain needed properties.
First, basis-dependent substitution distributes over linear combinations.

\begin{lemma}[Distributivity over linear combinations]\label{lem:distributiveSubstitution}
  For term combinations $\vec{t}_{i}$, a value combination $\vec{v}$, a
  variable $x$, coefficients $\alpha_i\in\C$, and a decoration $X$ such that
  $\ansubst{\vec v/x}{X}$ is defined:
  \(
    \Bigl(\sum_i \alpha_i\vec{t}_{i}\Bigr)\ansubst{\vec v/x}{X}
    \equiv
    \sum_i \alpha_i\vec{t}_{i} \ansubst{\vec v/x}{X}
  \).
  \qed
\end{lemma}

The next result states that substitution behaves consistently within each
equivalence class induced by the congruence $\equiv$.

\begin{lemma}[Compatibility with congruence]\label{lem:EquivSubstitutions}
  For value combinations $\vec{v},\vec{w}$, a term combination $\vec{t}$, and
  an decoration $X$ such that both
  $\ansubst{\vec{v}/x}{X}$ and $\ansubst{\vec{w}/x}{X}$ are defined:
  if $\vec{v}\equiv\vec{w}$, then
  $\vec{t}\ansubst{\vec{v}/x}{X}
  \equiv\vec{t}\ansubst{\vec{w}/x}{X}$.
  \qed
\end{lemma}

Note that this property does not extend across different bases; that is,
$\vec{t}\ansubst{\vec{v}/x}{X} \not\equiv\vec{t}\ansubst{\vec{v}/x}{Y}$.
For example,
\[
  (\Lam{x}{X}{y})\ansubst{\ket{+}/y}{\XB}
  = \Lam{x}{X}{\ket{+}} 
  \not\equiv
  \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big((\Lam{x}{C}{\ket{0}})
  + (\Lam{x}{X}{\ket{1}})\big)
  = (\Lam{x}{X}{y})\ansubst{\ket{+}/y}{\B}.
\]
This difference arises because the relation $\equiv$ does not commute with
lambda abstraction, nor with the case construct (indeed, the calculus is
intensional with respect to functions, since it is issued from a realisability
model).  Although the two terms are operationally equivalent, the calculus
distinguishes between the superposition of results, $\Lam{x}{X}{\alpha\vec{v}_{1}
+ \beta\vec{v}_{2}}$, and the superposition of functions,
$\alpha(\Lam{x}{X}{\vec{v}_{1}}) + \beta(\Lam{x}{X}{\vec{v}_{2}})$. 
This distinction reflects a physical intuition: the former corresponds to a
single experiment producing a superposition of outcomes, while the latter
represents a superposition of distinct experiments.

Finally, we introduce a convenient notation for generalised substitutions over
a term by closed values. A substitution $\sigma$ can be seen as a finite set of
individual substitutions applied consecutively to a term. Formally, for a term
$\vec{t}$, closed value combinations $\vec{v}_{1},\dots,\vec{v}_{n}$, variables
$x_1,\dots,x_n$, and decorations $X_1,\dots,X_n$:
\(
  \vec{t}\ansubst{\sigma}{}
  := \vec{t}\ansubst{\vec{v}_{1}/x_1}{X_1}\dotsb\ansubst{\vec{v}_{n}/x_n}{X_n}.
\)
Since each $\vec{v}_{i}$ is closed, the order of substitutions is irrelevant. We
regard $\sigma$ as a partial function from variables to pairs of closed value
combinations and bases (or $\AbsBasis$), and write $\dom{\sigma}$ for its domain.
The operation extends naturally: for a term $\vec{t}$, substitution $\sigma$,
a new variable $x\notin\dom{\sigma}$, value combination $\vec{v}$, and decoration $X$,
\(
  \vec{t}\ansubst{\sigma}{}\ansubst{\vec{v}/x}{X}
  = \vec{t}\ansubst{\sigma'}{}
\),
where $\sigma'$ extends $\sigma$ by mapping $x$ to $(\vec v,B)$. Likewise, two
disjoint substitutions $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$ can be merged:
\(
  \vec{t}\ansubst{\sigma_1}{}\ansubst{\sigma_2}{}
  = \vec{t}\ansubst{\sigma'}{}
\),
where $\dom{\sigma_1}\cap\dom{\sigma_2}=\emptyset$ and $\sigma'$ coincides with
$\sigma_i$ on $\dom{\sigma_i}$ for $i=1,2$.
